## Write your business case
- **The strategic bet. What specific outcome are you backing, who does it serve, and what is the mechanism that connects the product decision to a financial result?:** We're betting that a lightweight, offline-first foreman app will move 40% of field RFIs out of calls and texts and into Meridian within year one, cutting median time from a foreman's question to his answer by 50% against each project's own baseline, and that the resulting drop in crew delays and rework will give GC executives visible proof of value, securing renewals across our 38 enterprise accounts.
- **The assumptions. List the assumptions your case rests on, then rank them: which one, if wrong, most changes your conclusion?:** #1: Friction, not trust, keeps foremen off the platform. If wrong, nothing downstream happens. No adoption means no faster answers, no executive proof, no renewal effect. Every capability we invest in becomes infrastructure for a behavior that never arrives. This is the only assumption that invalidates the entire bet rather than weakening part of it, which is why the pilot tests it first.

#2: The 38 accounts' renewals are genuinely at risk if field adoption stays low. The brief never says the 38 top accounts are threatening to leave, it says mid-market is churning and field teams don't use the platform. If the top accounts would renew anyway, the app could succeed completely as a product and still produce no financial result. 

#3: The people answering RFIs will answer through Meridian. If foremen submit in the app but answers still come back by phone call, the winning aspiration breaks at its most important moment, and foremen will abandon the app because it only handles half the conversation.

#4: GC executives will see and credit the improvement to Meridian at renewal. If wrong, operational success exists but stays invisible at renewal time.
- **The expected return. What does the bet generate and when? Express it at unit level (per customer) and at scale (what volume hits target).:** Per account, the bet moves 40% of RFIs into the app and cuts each project's question-to-answer time by 50%, which generates retained ARR equal to the contract value multiplied by the reduction in that account's risk of not renewing, not the full contract. At scale, success means hitting 40% RFI adoption across all 38 accounts in year one, and the bet breaks even once it prevents enough non-renewals to cover its cost, which could be a single account if one top-100 contract exceeds the team's annual cost. Operational returns show up during the pilot and through year one, but financial returns lag until each account's next renewal, likely year two for most. The entire financial return depends on those renewals actually being at risk, which the brief never confirms.
- **The kill criterion. Name the specific metric, threshold, timeline, and financial consequence that tells the team to stop. Actionable, not a conversation.:** If fewer than 20% of RFIs at the two pilot accounts move into the app by week 12, while routing accuracy holds at or above 95% and offline sync works as designed, the Foundations product lead stops the bet at the week-12 review. The full build budget is not released, the team reallocation to backend and systems engineering is not committed, and the pilot app stays live in maintenance only. The next six weeks go to diagnosing whether trust, not friction, is the real barrier, and no RFI rebuild resumes until that diagnostic produces a new hypothesis. If the product itself underperforms, the team gets four weeks to fix it and one rerun of the test before the same consequences apply.

## Stress-test and finalize
- **Paste your finalized business case here.:** We are asking to fund a staged bet: a lightweight, offline-first mobile app that lets foremen and superintendents submit RFIs from the job site and receive answers back as easily as sending a text. Today, field teams at our 38 enterprise accounts barely use Meridian. Their questions travel by phone call and text, and answers return the same way, which means crews wait, work proceeds on guesses that become rework, and our platform holds an incomplete picture of what is happening on site. Our operational targets are to move 40% of field RFIs into Meridian within year one and cut the median time from a foreman's question to his answer by 50% against each project's own baseline. These are deliberate targets, not measured figures, and the staged funding below is designed to test them before significant capital is committed.

The financial logic runs through retention, not new revenue. The foreman uses the app, but the GC executive signs the renewal, so the bet only pays if the foreman's gain becomes visible to the buyer as reduced delays and rework across their projects. We model the return as retained gross profit: for each account, annual contract value multiplied by gross margin, by baseline non-renewal risk, and by the reduction in that risk the app produces, summed across all 38 accounts. We count only the renewal risk the app removes, not full contract value, and we weight by account rather than counting contracts saved, since accounts of very different sizes are not interchangeable.

Because the brief provides no contract values, margins, churn rates, or costs, every figure in this model other than the 38 accounts is illustrative and labeled as such. We assume a ten-person team at $200,000 fully loaded each, which puts total investment at $2.0 million, roughly $0.46 million for a 12-week pilot and $1.54 million for the full build, plus about $0.5 million a year to run the product after launch. We recommend a 24-month payback hurdle, measured from when retained profit begins flowing at renewal, since an 18-month hurdle would fail on renewal timing alone and a 30-month hurdle would be too lenient for a bet with a deliberately capped downside.

Under these assumptions, the results are sobering and worth stating plainly. In the base case, with $1.0 million average contracts, 80% gross margin, 10% annual non-renewal risk, and a 40% reduction in that risk, the app preserves about $1.22 million in gross profit a year, or $716,000 after run costs, for a payback of roughly 34 months, short of the hurdle. In a downside case, with $750,000 contracts, 75% margin, 7% risk, and a 25% reduction, it preserves about $374,000 a year, less than it costs to run. In a severe downside, it preserves about $53,000. Put differently, the full build reaches a 24-month payback on retention alone only if these accounts carry meaningful renewal risk, around 10% a year on roughly $1 million contracts, and field adoption cuts that risk roughly in half. The model is conservative, since a retained account keeps paying in later years, but the conclusion holds: retention economics justify the full build only under specific conditions we have not yet confirmed.

That makes the load-bearing assumption clear. Behaviorally, the bet depends on friction rather than trust being the reason foremen avoid the platform, because without adoption nothing downstream happens. Financially, it depends on these 38 renewals being genuinely at risk when field adoption stays low. Our brief confirms churn in the mid-market, not among enterprise accounts, so if the top accounts would renew regardless, the measurable return largely disappears. If confirmed figures fall short of the base case, this initiative should either be redesigned at lower cost or brought to the board explicitly as a strategic defense of our complexity premium against mobile-first competitors moving upmarket, rather than presented as a self-funding retention investment.

The ask is therefore staged behind two gates. We request funding for the 12-week pilot only, about $0.46 million. The first gate is behavioral: if fewer than 20% of RFIs at the two pilot accounts move into the app by week 12, while routing accuracy holds at or above 95% and offline sync works as designed, the Foundations product lead stops the bet at the week-12 review, and the pilot app stays live in maintenance only while the team spends six weeks diagnosing whether trust is the real barrier. If the product itself underperforms, the team gets four weeks to fix it and one rerun before the same consequence applies. The second gate is financial: even if the pilot succeeds, the full build is released only after finance and customer success confirm actual contract values, margins, and renewal risk for the 38 accounts, and the retention model clears the 24-month hurdle under the downside scenario using real data. If either gate fails, the $1.54 million full build and the planned team reallocation to backend and systems engineering remain uncommitted. The downside is capped at the pilot, and the full investment is released only when we have both behavioral and financial evidence that the bet works.
