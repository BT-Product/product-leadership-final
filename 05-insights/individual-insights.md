> Your personal reflection on building the strategy end to end.

## Friction points

_What was the hardest part of building this strategy, and how did you work through it?_

> I came into the Meridian case knowing almost nothing about construction. Partway through building the strategy, I had to stop and ask a basic question: what exactly is an RFI? Rather than treating that as a gap to hide, I treated it as the starting point for how I'd work. Without industry habit to lean on, I had to reason from the foreman's actual day: what he's doing when he hits a problem the drawings don't answer, who he calls, and what he's waiting on while work stalls. Before settling on RFI as our focus, I went back to the brief and checked what project managers and finance teams actually need from the field, so the choice rested on the case rather than on general assumptions. That first-principles lens ended up catching something important. When an early draft of our winning aspiration promised the foreman he would "receive" a response, I asked whether a confirmation that his RFI was received actually helped him. It didn't. He's still standing at the wall until a real answer arrives. That distinction between receipt and resolution reshaped the aspiration, our key results, and how the team charter defined ownership. Building in an unfamiliar industry forced me to ask the questions an insider might skip, and those questions turned out to be where the strategy got sharper.

## Key learnings

_A few surprising discoveries or insights you gained from the course overall._

> Before this course, I thought of a kill criterion as a safety net: a threshold that tells you when to stop. Evaluating the AI Bid Estimation Case showed me how easily that net can have holes in it. Its criterion looked complete, with a metric, a threshold, and a deadline, but the consequence rested on two undefined words. "Paused" didn't say whether the feature stayed live or what would restart it, and "reallocated" didn't say where the capacity would go. No owner was named, and the 7% threshold sat so close to the baseline that a lucky quarter could clear it. Each gap handed the decision back to the room at the exact moment the team would have the most incentive to argue for one more quarter. When I wrote the kill criterion for Meridian Foundations, I tried to close every one of those gaps: a specific metric, a 20% threshold, a week-12 deadline, a named owner, and consequences spelled out in advance, down to the full build budget staying unreleased and the team reallocation never happening. I also added a clause separating a product that failed from a hypothesis that failed, so the team would learn which one broke. The CFO's review pushed it further, asking for a financial gate alongside the behavioral one and for the dollars left uncommitted to be named. The learning I'm taking forward: a good kill criterion doesn't schedule a debate. It ends one before it starts.

## "Aha!" moment

_Your main "aha" moment during the project process._

> My biggest aha moment came while stress-testing the AI Bid Estimation Case. Its headline looked airtight: an upsell rate rising from 6% to 8%, $896,000 in new revenue, and a 2.4-month payback on a $180,000 build. Then I noticed the baseline. At 6%, 24 of those 32 upsells would have happened anyway, so the feature was really responsible for only 8, and the payback stretched to nearly ten months. That alone was surprising, but what changed how I think was what happened when I tested the rate itself. If the lift came in 20% smaller, payback stretched to about a year. If the 8% target missed by 20%, payback stretched to about four years. Miss it by 25%, and the return disappeared entirely. A shift of less than two percentage points in a single assumption was the difference between a quick win and a bet that never pays back. That's when I understood why: the entire return lived in a small gap between two numbers, so a tiny miss on one of them erased most of the value. Even the kill criterion was caught in it, since its 7% threshold sat so close to the baseline that ordinary quarter-to-quarter noise could clear it. The aha I'm carrying forward: before committing to any bet, find the assumption the whole return depends on and ask what happens if it's even slightly wrong. I applied that directly to Meridian, ranking our assumptions by how much a miss would change the conclusion and building base, downside, and severe scenarios instead of a single hopeful number.
